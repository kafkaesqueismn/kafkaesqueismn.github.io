The BIOS password I couldn't find

27 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

  • hardware
  • bios
  • reverse-engineering
  • firmware

I have a Lenovo V110-15AST (model 80TD, AMD A9, 2018) sitting on my desk that I can't get into. It's locked behind a BIOS supervisor password I don't have: no setup menu, no boot device selection, nothing. This is a write-up of how I went after it, where I got stuck, and what I'd try next. I haven't beaten it yet, so treat this as a lab notebook rather than a victory lap.

It's my own machine, which is the only reason any of this is in scope. "Recover a password I'm not supposed to have" and "recover a password on hardware I own" are very different sentences, and only one of them belongs on a portfolio.

The lock

A supervisor password is the strong one. A power-on password gates boot; a supervisor password gates the firmware configuration itself, and on most machines you can't clear it from software or by pulling the CMOS battery, which only resets settings, not the password. So the interesting question isn't "how do I click past this," it's "where does this secret physically live, and can I reach it?"

My working assumption at the start, the obvious one, was that the password, or a hash of it, sits in the main BIOS SPI flash. That assumption is exactly what the rest of this post is about.

Dumping the flash (the part that worked)

The BIOS lives on an SOIC-8 SPI flash chip on the motherboard. To read it I used a CH341A programmer with an SOIC-8 clip, driving it two ways: AsProgrammer as a GUI on Windows, and flashrom as a cross-check. (I installed the CH341 drivers for the programmer to enumerate at all, which on modern Windows is its own small adventure.)

The read itself was the cleanest part of the whole project. I pulled a full dump of the chip and, this is the step people skip, read it a second time and compared the two images to confirm they were identical, so I knew I had a faithful copy and not a flaky clip contact. At that point I had a byte-for-byte image of the firmware and felt pretty good about it.

That feeling did not last.

The wall

With the dump in hand, I went looking for the password. And I couldn't find it.

I searched the image for anything that looked like a stored credential, read through forum threads and guides for this family of machine, and tried to map the layout of the dump to figure out which region would even hold a password. I came up with nothing I could act on. I had a perfect copy of the firmware and no idea which bytes mattered.

It's worth being precise about what "couldn't find it" means, because there are at least two very different explanations and I can't yet rule either out:

  1. It isn't in this chip. The secret may simply not live in the main SPI flash.
  2. It is in this chip, but not as anything I'd recognise. A supervisor password is far more likely to be stored as a hash, or inside a structured NVRAM variable region, than as searchable plaintext. "I grepped the image and found nothing" is weak evidence that something isn't there; it's strong evidence only that it isn't there in the clear.

I don't think I did enough to separate those two cases, and that's the honest gap in the work so far.

The eBay detour

While that was stalled, I tried the blunt approach: if the password lives on the board, replace the board. A board swap isn't a clever bypass, but it's a legitimate one when it's your own hardware: you're physically removing the component that holds the secret.

The replacement I bought off eBay turned out to be a different revision of the board for this model, and it didn't play nicely with the rest of the machine. Finding the correct revision for this exact laptop proved genuinely hard: this is a budget 2018 machine, and specific-revision spares for it aren't exactly a thriving market. That's where the project is parked.

Why I shelved it (and what I'd do differently)

I stopped because I'd run out of informed moves. I could keep buying boards and hoping, but that's spending money to avoid thinking, and I'd rather come back to it with a hypothesis I can actually test.

Here's what I'd investigate next, framed as questions rather than answers:

  • Is the secret even on the main chip? On a lot of laptops, security state lives outside the main BIOS flash: in the embedded controller (EC), or in a small dedicated EEPROM. If that's the case here, then dumping the BIOS chip and searching it was always going to lead nowhere, and I'd want to identify the EC and whatever sits near it before touching the SPI flash again. I want to be clear that this is a theory that fits the symptom, not something I've confirmed on this board.
  • If it is on the main chip, what form is it in? Instead of searching for a plaintext string, I'd try to identify the NVRAM/variable region in the dump and understand its structure, then compare a locked image against a known-unlocked one to see which bytes actually change. A diff is worth a thousand greps.
  • Verify before escalating. Before I buy another board, I'd confirm which chip holds the password, so I'm replacing the right thing on purpose rather than by elimination.

The thing I actually took away from this isn't about Lenovo. It's that I'd quietly assumed the answer was in the chip I knew how to read, and then spent my effort there because that's where the light was. The hardware skills (clipping onto an SOIC-8, dumping and verifying an image) were the easy, teachable part. The part I got wrong was a reasoning error: I let a convenient assumption stand in for an investigated one. That's the bit I want to fix when I pick this back up.
