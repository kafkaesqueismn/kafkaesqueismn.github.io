Entropy alone flags a quarter of popular domains

12 Nov 2026 · 4 min read

  • dns
  • detection
  • blue-team
  • python

My PCAP analyser flags DNS lookups that look machine-generated, because that's what malware using a DGA (domain generation algorithm) looks like on the wire. It's also a sign of DNS tunnelling, where data is hidden in subdomains.

The usual approach is Shannon entropy: random strings have high entropy, real words don't. While building it I noticed that stackoverflow scores 3.55 bits per character, which is above the 3.3 threshold I was using. So I made the check require two of these four signals:

  • entropy ≥ 3.3
  • vowels make up under 20% of the characters
  • letters mixed with at least 2 digits
  • a run of 5 or more consonants

Only labels of 10+ characters are checked, because short strings can't reach high entropy even when they're random.

I'd tested that on a few examples. This time I wanted proper numbers.

Real domains

I took the top 100,000 sites from the Tranco list, a research ranking of popular domains. For each one I used the main label (google from google.com) and kept the ones with 10+ characters. That left 33,097 labels.

CheckPopular domains flagged
Entropy ≥ 3.3 alone7,624 (23.0%)
Two of four signals1,609 (4.9%)

Entropy alone flags almost a quarter of popular domains. Some of what it caught:

sharepoint.com, googlesyndication.com, microsoftonline.com, windowsupdate.com, google-analytics.com, creativecommons.org

That would be useless on a real network. Your alerts would be full of Microsoft.

The two-signal rule cuts that to 4.9%. What's left are mostly names with digits or abbreviations: appsflyersdk.com, crashlytics.com, spotifycdn.com, flightradar24.com, privacy-mgmt.com. A few really are machine-generated, like w2rt6byl5nf1opmc-a.com, which is some kind of CDN or tracking domain. Still too noisy to alert on by itself, which is why the README says to keep an allow-list.

Generated domains

Then the other side: how much fake DGA-style stuff each check catches. I generated 5,000 labels of each kind:

Kind of labelEntropy aloneTwo signals
random a–z, 10–16 chars45.7%66.3%
random a–z, exactly 10 chars13.3%45.2%
random a–z and 0–9, 10–16 chars61.8%87.7%
hex, 16 chars37.8%90.7%
base32, 16 chars (tunnel-style)92.5%95.4%
two dictionary words joined25.8%2.5%

I didn't expect the two-signal rule to come out ahead on both sides. It flags fewer real domains and catches more random ones. Entropy on a 10–16 character string is just a noisy measurement. A random 10-letter string only goes over 3.3 about 13% of the time, because there aren't enough characters for the distribution to spread out. The vowel and consonant checks don't have that problem.

Hex is the clearest example. Only 16 possible characters means entropy tops out at 4 bits, and short hex strings usually land under 3.3. But hex is almost always low on vowels and full of digits, so two of the other signals fire straight away.

The one that gets past

The last row is the one that matters. Some real malware families build domains from dictionary words instead of random characters, like bluebooksantiago or harshenlawmake. These look like English to every character-level check, and the two-signal rule catches 2.5% of them. That's less often than it flags real popular domains.

No per-name rule is going to catch those, because there's nothing about one name on its own that gives it away.

What does work

Looking at behaviour across many lookups instead of one name at a time:

  • Lots of NXDOMAIN. A DGA tries hundreds of domains and only a few are actually registered, so an infected host produces a burst of "this domain doesn't exist" answers. Dictionary-word domains don't help with that.
  • Lots of unique subdomains under one domain. This is the strongest sign of tunnelling. Tools like iodine and dnscat2 put a new chunk of data in a new subdomain on every query. In my sample capture, one tunnel chunk happened to encode to wubwauyxihozjpza, which passed the per-name check, but the per-domain count caught it anyway.
  • A spike in TXT queries, since TXT records carry the most data back.

So the per-name check is fine as one weak signal among several, but it isn't a detector by itself. The stackoverflow thing was just the first sign of that.
